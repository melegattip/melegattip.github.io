The problem

Managing personal finances in Argentina has its quirks: installment purchases that commit future months, income and savings split between pesos and dollars, and recurring expenses that are easy to lose track of. Spreadsheets get abandoned, and apps built for other markets don't model installments.

Niloft covers that journey: logging expenses and income, tracking pending installments, setting budgets and savings goals, and getting an analysis of where the money goes.

The approach

  • From microservices to a modular monolith

    The first version was 4 REST microservices: users, engine, AI and gamification. For a one-person team, four deployments and more than one database were cost without benefit. I consolidated them into a Go monolith with domain modules and a single PostgreSQL database.

  • Modules that don't know each other

    Each module — authentication, transactions, savings, recurring items, analytics, AI — exposes its own routes and talks to the rest through an internal event bus. Events are always published after persisting.

  • Multi-tenant from the data model up

    Shared accounts with roles and permissions, an audit log and per-tenant isolation. Authentication with JWT and a second factor.

  • AI to read the numbers

    An AI module builds the monthly analysis and recommendations from the user's transactions, so the data doesn't end up as just a chart.

What I learned

  • Removing is also designing

    Gamification — points and achievements — looked good but helped no one get their finances in order. I replaced it with a simpler financial level and deleted the whole module.

  • In progress

    Subscriptions with plans, and the Android app.
